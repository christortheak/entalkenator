[Judge Maralla]: Good morning. The court will hear argument this morning in number 25-581, St. Mary Catholic Parish versus Roy. This is an appeal from the district court in Colorado, which was affirmed by the Tenth Circuit. I am Judge Maralla, and I am joined on the panel by Judge Holland and Judge Patterson. We have read the briefs, and read them closely, so counsel need not spend time reciting the facts. We will begin with the appellants. Mr. Rassbach, welcome. Please proceed when you are ready.

[Appellant's Attorney]: May it please the court. Colorado has promised every four-year-old in the state fifteen hours a week of free preschool, at the school the family chooses, whether public, private, or faith-based. The Catholic parish preschools of the Archdiocese of Denver are the one group of otherwise eligible providers that cannot take part. They cannot take part because the State requires them to give up a religious practice at the center of their mission, which is forming children in the Catholic faith in partnership with parents who share it.

That is the question before the court, and I would frame it this way. Colorado is not required to fund private preschools at all. Having chosen to do so, may it condition that benefit on a religious school abandoning how it defines its own religious community? The Supreme Court has answered that question in Carson against Makin, and the answer turns on what the program does to religious exercise, not on whether the State uses the word religion.

I rest on three grounds, and any one of them is sufficient. First, Carson itself. The Tenth Circuit confined Carson to programs that exclude schools on an explicit religious basis. But Maine did not exclude every religious school either. The Court looked to how the program operated, not to how it was labeled. Second, even if Smith governs, the mandate is not generally applicable. Colorado permits secular enrollment preferences that undercut the same equal-access interest it invokes against my clients, and the Department decides, case by case, which preferences are acceptable. The program director testified that she would approve preferences for gender-nonconforming children, for children of color, and for the LGBTQ community. Third, on this record Colorado cannot satisfy strict scrutiny. The Department has never received a single complaint of this kind in the decades these preschools have been licensed, and its own portal offers less restrictive ways to protect families.

I want to be clear about what we do not ask. We do not ask this court to overrule Smith. And if the court prefers the narrowest path, the second ground resolves the case without deciding how far Carson reaches. I will take these grounds in whatever order the court prefers, and I welcome the court's questions.

[Judge Holland]: Mr. Rassbach, I would like to start with your first ground. You say you do not ask us to overrule Smith, and of course this court could not if you did. But you read Carson to say that any condition on a public benefit that pressures a religious practice calls for strict scrutiny, even when the condition is neutral on its face. Where does Carson actually hold that? And if it does, what is left of Smith for benefit programs?

[Appellant's Attorney]: Your Honor, Carson does not say it in those words. At pages 785 and 789 it holds that a State may not deny a benefit based on religious exercise, however the benefit and restriction are described. Sherbert, Thomas, and Hobbie applied that principle to neutral conditions. Smith remains for generally applicable laws that burden religion incidentally. And if the court doubts my reading, the general applicability ground resolves this case without it.

[Judge Maralla]: Mr. Rassbach, you offer general applicability as the narrower path, so let me test it. Your categorical exemption argument depends on the preferences for children with disabilities and for low-income families being exceptions to the mandate. Both courts below read the Colorado statute to say they are not exceptions at all. We ordinarily give real weight to how lower federal courts read a state statute. Why should we not accept that reading? And if we do, what is left of that argument?

[Appellant's Attorney]: Your Honor, the court should give that reading real weight, and if it accepts it, the categorical argument is weaker. I will not pretend otherwise. But it is not gone, because comparability is a federal question. Under Tandon, the inquiry is whether the permitted conduct undermines the State's interest in a similar way, not what the State chooses to call it. A preschool open only to low-income families gives every other family unequal access based on a characteristic the mandate lists. And whatever the court decides about that reading, the catchall and Ms. Odean's testimony stand on their own, because they do not depend on it.

[Judge Patterson]: Mr. Rassbach, you said the catchall and Ms. Odean's testimony stand on their own, so let me test that. The regulation says on its face that no preference may violate the mandate. The courts below found that no one at the Department claimed power to waive it. And the record shows no preference actually granted that departs from it. Ms. Odean was answering hypotheticals. Under Fulton, is a director's mistaken guess about what the statute would allow enough to make the catchall a mechanism for individualized exemptions? Or do you have to show she actually had the power to grant one?

[Appellant's Attorney]: Your Honor, the question is authority, and I accept that a mistaken guess alone would not be enough. But the authority is here. Under the regulation, the Department decides whether a requested preference complies with the mandate, and Ms. Odean is the official who runs that process. That is a decision about which reasons for departing from equal access are acceptable. Fulton at 537 holds that the power to make that decision is enough, whether or not an exception has yet been granted. Her testimony shows the standard she applies, which is historical disadvantage. Colorado now calls that standard a mistake, but it is the standard of the person who decides, and under it the Department has refused my clients while testifying that it could approve preferences for other groups.

[Judge Patterson]: Mr. Rassbach, I want to understand the limit on that. In Fulton, the contract said in terms that the Commissioner could grant exceptions in her sole discretion. Here the Department decides whether a requested preference complies with the statute. Every agency that enforces a rule decides, request by request, whether conduct complies. If that power is enough, what law is generally applicable? And if it is not enough, what in this record shows the Department could waive the mandate, as opposed to misreading it?

[Appellant's Attorney]: Your Honor, I accept that deciding whether conduct complies with a fixed rule is ordinary enforcement, and that alone would not defeat general applicability. The line is whether the decision applies the rule's own content or weighs which reasons for departing from it are worthy. Here the standard Ms. Odean described, historical disadvantage, appears nowhere in the mandate, and she uses it to decide which preferences are allowed. As to the record, I cannot point to a granted waiver that Colorado concedes departs from the mandate. What the record shows is the regulation's own approved example, a preference for families receiving housing assistance, which sits against the listed characteristic of lack of housing, and the Tenth Circuit's observation that her examples would certainly seem to violate the requirement. That is a mechanism for value judgments, not a simple misreading.

[Judge Maralla]: Thank you, Mr. Rassbach. Your time has expired. Ms. Norton, we will hear from you for the appellees. You will want to address the director's testimony and the housing assistance example, which the panel has been asking about. Please proceed.

[Appellee's Attorney]: May it please the court. My name is Helen Norton, and I represent the State of Colorado.

I would like to begin by framing what this case is about, because it shapes everything the panel has asked. Colorado opened its universal preschool program to Catholic schools and invited them in. The program pays for religious education, religious curriculum, and worship in the school day. What it asks of every participating preschool, public or private, secular or religious, is a single thing: that no child be turned away because of the family's race, religion, sexual orientation, gender identity, housing, income, or disability. Petitioners chose not to join, and they chose that on instructions the Archdiocese gave in January of 2023. More than forty faith-based providers did join in the first year, including six Catholic Charities preschools of the Archdiocese itself. So the question is not whether Colorado may take something from religious schools. It is whether the Constitution gives religious providers a right to be paid with public funds on terms that no other provider receives.

Judge Maralla asked me to address the director's testimony and the housing assistance example, and I will take them first.

On the director's testimony. Counsel is correct that Ms. Odean testified that the catchall could allow preferences for gender-nonconforming children, for children of color, and for the LGBTQ community. The Tenth Circuit observed that such preferences would seem to violate the statute. She was mistaken, and the State does not defend her answer. But Judge Patterson put the right question, which is whether she had the power to grant such an exemption, and the record answers it. She repeatedly disclaimed any authority to waive the statute. So did Dr. Roy, in her letter to the faith-based providers and again in her deposition. So did Ms. Cooke. Counsel acknowledged this morning that he cannot point to a single preference granted that departs from the mandate. In the first year the Department approved seventeen requests, and none departed from it. In Fulton, the contract said in terms that the Commissioner could grant exceptions in her sole discretion. Nothing like that exists here. And the regulation permits no preference that violates the statute. A rule that conflicts with a statute is void under Colorado law, so if the Department ever did approve such a preference, the remedy would be to invalidate it, not to extend it to everyone. A mistaken answer to a hypothetical question is not a power to waive.

On housing assistance. Three points. First, this argument appeared for the first time in Petitioners' merits brief, and the record contains no evidence of how any provider has used that example. Second, giving priority to families who receive housing assistance does not exclude any family because it lacks housing. Those benefits exist to serve families without stable housing, so the preference points toward them, not away. Third, even if it were read as a proxy for low income, that brings us to the categorical argument, and there I agree with counsel that it is weaker than he made it sound. Both courts below read the statute to treat specialized services for disabled and low-income children as part of equal opportunity, not as exceptions to it. That reading deserves real weight. And even if the panel treated those preferences as exceptions, comparability under Tandon is judged against the State's interest, which is that no family loses an available publicly funded choice because of protected status. Services for children who need them advance that interest. Petitioners' request would defeat it.

Counsel also described this as a case where Carson reaches covert burdens, and I agree that labels do not control. But Carson concerned a State that excluded schools because of what they taught religiously. Colorado funds what these schools teach. The condition here falls on every provider alike.

And if strict scrutiny applied, the record shows Petitioners' own witnesses would refuse the children of same-sex parents, and Colorado has no less restrictive way to prevent exclusion.

I welcome the court's questions.

[Judge Patterson]: Ms. Norton, thank you. Let me give you a hypothetical. A Jewish preschool wants to fill its seats first with Jewish families, and a Catholic preschool wants to fill its seats first with Catholic families. Religious affiliation is a protected characteristic in the mandate. As Colorado reads the mandate today, may either school do that and still receive UPK funds? And if the answer is no, tell me why a rule that bars a church school from preferring its own members is not a burden that falls only on religious providers.

[Appellee's Attorney]: No, Your Honor. As Colorado reads the mandate today, neither school may give priority based on religious affiliation and still receive UPK funds. The congregation preference was removed because the statute does not allow it. But the requirement binds every provider. A secular school may not prefer families by religion, race, or sexual orientation either. That religious schools are the ones most likely to seek the preference does not make the rule one that targets them.

[Judge Maralla]: Ms. Norton, let me take Judge Patterson's question one step further. I understand the Department once had a provision that would have governed the hiring decisions of faith-based providers, and that it disavowed and removed it after this suit was filed. Why? If the answer is that the State could not constitutionally condition funding on whom a church school chooses to employ, then what distinguishes the choice of which families join the school community?

[Appellee's Attorney]: Your Honor, the record shows that the Department disavowed and removed that provision after suit was filed. I do not read the record as a concession by the State that such a condition would be unconstitutional, and the provision is not before the court. But there is a real distinction. Hiring concerns who carries out a church's ministry, and this Court's church autonomy decisions, Hosanna-Tabor and Our Lady of Guadalupe, rest on a separate doctrine. Enrollment is different. It is the very service the State is paying for, and the question is whether a child may be turned away from it because of who the child's parents are.

[Judge Maralla]: You say enrollment is the service the State is paying for. The Archdiocese says the service is forming a child in the faith in partnership with parents who share it. So let me test where the line falls. A school admits every child, whatever the parents' religion or orientation, but asks every family to sign a statement supporting Catholic teaching. Does the mandate forbid that condition? And if it does, how is Colorado not dictating the content of the program it says it welcomes?

[Appellee's Attorney]: Not as such, Your Honor. The mandate does not forbid a school from telling every family what it teaches and asking them to respect it. What it forbids is refusing a child, or ending enrollment, because of the family's religion or sexual orientation. So the question is whether a statement operates as that kind of refusal, and that depends on how it is applied. Here the record shows that Petitioners' own witnesses would turn away the children of same-sex parents. And Colorado does not dictate the content of the program. The curriculum, the worship, and the teaching remain entirely the school's.

[Judge Holland]: Ms. Norton, you distinguish Carson because Colorado funds what these schools teach. But Maine did not bar every religious school either. It barred those that taught through the lens of their faith, and it kept the rest. Colorado keeps the religious schools that will admit any family, and bars those that run their community by their faith. Setting labels aside, how is that different? Or is your position that Carson reaches only a rule that names religion?

[Appellee's Attorney]: No, Your Honor. Carson is not limited to a rule that names religion, and Lukumi makes that clear. The difference lies in the trigger. Maine disqualified a school because of how it taught: if faith shaped the instruction, the school was out. Colorado asks nothing about whether a school's teaching is religious. A Catholic school that teaches through the lens of its faith stays in, and a secular school that turns families away on the same grounds is out. The disqualifying act is the refusal, whoever commits it. So the line follows conduct that any provider could engage in, and not religious exercise.

[Judge Maralla]: Thank you, Ms. Norton. Your time has expired. The court thanks both counsel for their briefs and for their candid answers this morning. The case is submitted, and we are adjourned.
