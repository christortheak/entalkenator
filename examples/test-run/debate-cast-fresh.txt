[Janson Skullbottom]: Thank you, Hillford. And I'll take "most of the time" as the kindest review you've given me in years.

Let me begin with a concession, because Dana has earned it. Courts write badly for the public. The tenant facing eviction, the parent in a custody fight: these people have more riding on a judicial opinion than anyone in this room, and they are the least able to read one. Dana is right about the problem. My quarrel is entirely with the cure.

Here is what the proposal actually does. It creates a second text of the law. That text speaks in the court's voice but carries none of the court's force, and it is written for exactly the readers least equipped to tell the difference. That is a well-meant mistake.

Take the three things the summary is supposed to say. First, who won. Picture a tenant who reads that her case was reversed and remanded. Did she win? Maybe. Or maybe she just gets another hearing, which she could lose. Appellate outcomes are partial, mixed, and hedged, and often the most important thing in an opinion is what the court declined to decide. That is the first thing three hundred words will cut.

Second, what the court decided. Choosing how broadly to state a holding is itself a judicial act. If the summary puts it a little more broadly than the opinion does, which version does that frightened tenant believe?

Third, what the decision means for people in the same position. That isn't a summary. That's advice. It asks a court to predict how its ruling applies to facts that were never in front of it, which is precisely what courts are built not to do.

[Janson Skullbottom]: That's a fair hit, Hillford, and I'll take it straight. Yes. A plain sentence in the opinion binds, and a careless one binds carelessly.
